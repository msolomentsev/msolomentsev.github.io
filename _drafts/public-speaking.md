Guides to public speaking often begin with Aristotle's modes of persuasion: ethos (credibility); pathos (emotions); logos (logic). I find this framework to be objectively true, but practically unhelpful. This framework basically implies that each of the three modes should be considered equally. To an average person trying to improve their speaking, this immediately rings false: A very compelling public speaker (ethos) can get away with a lot of failures in other respects. For better and for worse, being a truly excellent presenter is much more valuable than improving the content of what you're saying.

Moreover, people generally are trying to improve their speaking for a particular situation (a pitch, a work presentation, a best man speech, etc.), and generally know what they are going to say. What is intimidating and difficult is the act of presenting to an audience.

Typical advice here is again not incorrect but insufficient. Eye contact, confidence, posture, and the like are all important. Rather, I think the critical thing is to have a sort of accurate mental model for successful presenting, and that's what I aim to present below. 

A presentation (whatever the specific case may be), is two things: A presentation is a **conversation**; a presentation is a **performance**. At first, these might seem like two different frameworks for public speaking, but I hope to convince you that they are two different perspectives at looking at the same task. Moreover, if you're able to internalize both viewpoints and then hold both in your mind at once -- like crossing your eyes when looking at one of those magic eye images -- you'll really be an excellent presenter.

### Conversation

A conversation is a normal, everyday act. It is casual. Most of the time, you don't need to prepare or mentally steel yourself for any particular one (this may be more or less true depending on your social skills/position on the autism spectrum). There are many things I like to extend from conversation to presentation, but the most important is mindset: a successful presentation is relaxed and comfortable! 

Of course, that doesn't mean that the presenter themselves is at ease. Every time before I present or pitch in front of an audience, I get quite nervous! But when I start speaking, I force myself to be relaxed and calm - to slow down meaningfully. Perhaps the most meaningful compliment I was ever given about my personal public speaking was after accepting an award at a pitch competition. It was a sort of unfamiliar and weird environment, and I certainly didn't know exactly what I was supposed to say in that situation. Afterward, a friend told me that she felt my speech set the room at ease - that it was calming. That's incredibly high praise!

OK. How do we achieve this? Obviously, inarguably, the best thing is practice in front of real audiences. I can't help you there, but your local toastmasters club might. That's generic but objectively true advice. What else? For me, the easiest thing to do is to pretend you are having a conversation with someone in the audience. Pick a person, preferably someone friendly or known to you, and simply talk to them. This is sub-ideal from an eye contact perspective, but very nice from a comfort perspective. If your conversation partner laughs, you should pause! If they look confused, slow down or re-explain yourself. A speech is just a conversation! Act like it.
